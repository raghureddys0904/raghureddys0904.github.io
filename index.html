A celebration of Shakti

Navratri

Nine nights of devotion, dance and light. Navratri is a major Hindu festival honouring the divine feminine power, celebrated with prayer, music, colourful traditions and community gatherings.

The festival

What is Navratri?

The word Navratri comes from Sanskrit: nava means “nine” and ratri means “night.” The festival is observed on several occasions during the year, with the most widely celebrated autumn observance known as Sharad Navratri.

Across India, devotees worship Goddess Durga and her forms, visit temples, offer prayers, fast according to family or regional customs, and gather for cultural celebrations. The dates follow the Hindu lunar calendar, so they change each year on the Gregorian calendar.

Faith & symbolism

The meaning behind the celebration

Navratri honours Shakti—the divine feminine energy—and celebrates the victory of good over evil. Stories and customs differ across regions, but devotion, courage, renewal and community are common themes.

Worship of the Goddess

Many devotees pray to Durga and her nine forms, seeking strength, wisdom, protection and well-being.

Good triumphs over evil

The festival recalls divine strength overcoming destructive forces and encourages people to choose truth and goodness.

Community & renewal

Music, dance, shared meals and gatherings help families and neighbours celebrate together.

Did you know? In many parts of eastern India, the final days of Navratri overlap with Durga Puja, featuring elaborate artistic displays and community worship. In Gujarat, evening Garba and Dandiya Raas are especially popular.
Nine nights, many traditions

How the nine nights are observed

In traditions that honour the Navadurga, each day is associated with a form of Goddess Durga. Names, rituals and interpretations can vary by community.

Traditions across India

How people celebrate

  • Prayer and puja: Homes and temples are decorated, lamps are lit, and prayers or devotional songs are offered.
  • Garba and Dandiya: In Gujarat and elsewhere, people dance in circles to rhythmic music, sometimes using decorated sticks for Dandiya Raas.
  • Fasting and food: Some devotees fast or choose special meals; practices vary by household and region.
  • Regional customs: In parts of South India, families arrange a display of dolls called Golu or Bommai Kolu. In West Bengal, Durga Puja is marked by pandals, art and community worship.
  • Vijayadashami / Dussehra: The festival period concludes in many traditions with a celebration of victory and a fresh start.

Colour, rhythm and devotion

In a nutshell

Navratri is more than a festival: it is a time for faith, inner strength, cultural expression and community. Whether celebrated through quiet prayer, a lively Garba night, Golu displays or Durga Puja, its message is one of hope and the triumph of good.
